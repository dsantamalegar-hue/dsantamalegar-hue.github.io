Glossary of Logic and Argumentation

Argument
A set of claims where some of them (the premises) are intended to support another claim (the conclusion).
Argument Diagram
Also called an argument map or argument tree; a diagram showing each premise and the conclusion represented by nodes. Arrows link nodes to show the direction of support, indicating whether support is independent or dependent.
Conclusion
The sentence in an argument of which the speaker wants to persuade their audience.
Conditional Probability
The probability of the conclusion given the truth of the premises.
Counterexample
A situation in which the premises are true but the conclusion is nevertheless false. The existence of a counterexample makes a deductive argument invalid.
Declarative Sentence
A sentence that makes an assertion—a claim that can be true or false.
Deductive Argument
An argument claiming that if the premises are true, then one must, as a matter of logical necessity, accept the conclusion. It leaves no room for mere probability.
Defeater
Additional evidence that, if added to the premises, would change the assessment and defeat the reasons for accepting the conclusion.
Dependent Support
Provided by a premise when it is not sufficient on its own to support the conclusion but requires other premises to do so.
Explanation
Reasoning where the author assumes the claim is already accepted and aims to clarify why it is so.
Extended Argument
A larger argument where the conclusion of a smaller argument (intermediate conclusion) serves as a premise for the larger one.
Extraneous Material
Background, rhetoric, or digressions that do not add content to the argument and should be omitted in standard form reconstruction.
Implicit Material
Content that is part of the argument but left unstated because the speaker believes the context makes it obvious. These should be made explicit in reconstruction.
Independent Support
Provided by a premise when it is sufficient by itself to effectively support the conclusion.
Indexical
A context-sensitive term (e.g., I, you, here, now) whose reference shifts with the speaker, time, or place.
Indicator Words
Words that signal a conclusion (conclusion indicators) or a premise (premise indicators) is about to appear.
Inductive Argument
An argument claiming that if the premises are true, then the conclusion is more probable than not. It does not provide a logical guarantee.
Inductive Force
An argument has inductive force if it is not deductively valid, but assuming the premises are true, it is more reasonable than not that the conclusion is true.
Inductive Soundness
An argument is inductively sound (or cogent) if it is inductively forceful and all of its premises are actually true.
Inference
Any step from premises to conclusion within an argument.
Principle of Charity
The requirement to reconstruct an argument in its strongest and clearest version consistent with the context.
Proposition
The factual content expressed by an assertive sentence on a particular occasion.
Rational Persuasiveness
An argument is rationally persuasive if it is valid/forceful, the premises are reasonable to believe, and it is not defeated by outside information.
Soundness
A property of a deductive argument that is both valid and has true premises.
Standard Form
A device that lists premises, draws an inference bar, and places the conclusion beneath it for easy evaluation.
Validity
A property of a deductive argument where it is impossible for all premises to be true and the conclusion to be false.

Key Concepts in Reasoning

  • Inference to the Best Explanation (IBE): Evaluates competing hypotheses to conclude that the explanation best accounting for data is probably true.
  • Analogical Arguments: Infer that because two things are similar in certain respects, they are likely similar in others. These are inductive and defeasible.
  • Causal Reasoning: Relies on causal generalizations; understanding necessary and sufficient conditions is essential.

Common Fallacies and Ploys

  • Formal Fallacies: Structural errors like Affirming the Consequent or Denying the Antecedent.
  • Ad Hominem: Attacking the person rather than their reasons.
  • Straw Target: Misrepresenting an opponent's position to make it easier to refute.
  • Begging the Question: Circular reasoning where the conclusion is assumed in the premises.
  • Appeal to Fear/Novelty/Identity: Rhetorical ploys that manipulate emotions or psychology instead of providing rational support.
